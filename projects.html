Building a SIEM to Watch My Own Front Door

What I built, what it caught, and what it taught me about leaving things public

The Setup

This started as a class project — my Linux professor let us build anything we found genuinely interesting, and mine turned into a full Wazuh SIEM deployment: manager, indexer, and dashboard running on terminus, paired with a Cowrie SSH honeypot on a fresh Oracle Cloud ARM instance I spun up specifically for this, kept isolated from anything I actually cared about protecting. The professor liked it enough to suggest I bring it to the department's cybersecurity showcase, so what started as a class assignment became something I actually had to explain to other people.

What It Caught

Cowrie ran for a little over a week. In that time, it logged somewhere around 3,000 attempts — mostly SSH, with a handful of HTTPS/TLS connections oddly aimed at port 22 as well. Nothing about it looked targeted or manual; it read exactly like automated scanning, the kind of background noise that's apparently just constantly hitting anything with a public IP. Sources were scattered globally, no single region dominating once I had GeoIP enrichment wired in. The one pattern that did stand out: activity clustered noticeably around early morning hours on my end — whether that says something about attacker time zones or just how bot scheduling works, I don't fully know.

Tuning Out the Noise

Out of the box, Wazuh alerts on everything, and between Cowrie's constant honeypot traffic and my own routine SSH/Pi-hole activity, that meant a flood of alerts within the first day that told me almost nothing useful. I wrote suppression rules filtering by Cowrie-specific traffic so the noise from my own legitimate activity stopped burying anything worth actually looking at. It's not a glamorous part of the project, but it's the difference between a SIEM that alerts and a SIEM you can actually use.

What Broke

The honest answer: my own hardware. Running the full Wazuh stack — manager, indexer, and dashboard — on terminus started eating enough RAM that it slowed down everything else on that box, including its job as my Tailscale exit node, which meant it was dragging down my other devices too. Once the showcase was over, I shut it down for exactly that reason — no analyst benefit left to justify the resource cost.

What I'd Do Differently

Run it on more capable hardware, and wait until I actually had more public-facing infrastructure worth monitoring before building the SIEM around it. A single honeypot on an isolated box gets you interesting attack data, but a real deployment earns its keep watching things that actually matter.

What It Actually Changed

This is the part that stuck with me most. I'd run a public-facing website on a Raspberry Pi for a while before this project — no real hardening, no monitoring, updates whenever I remembered. It never got hacked. But I also had no idea what "never got hacked" actually meant, because I'd never looked. Watching real attack traffic hit a box in real time, at the volume it did, over just a week, made the abstract idea of "the internet is hostile by default" into something I'd actually seen with my own logs. I don't leave things running unmonitored and unpatched anymore — not because I got burned, but because I finally understand what "fine, so far" was actually resting on.

Cowrie's retired now. That same Oracle box runs this site instead.
